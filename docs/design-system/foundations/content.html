Foundation

Content

Hewn talks like a calm colleague who has the facts: what happened, to what, what it means for your work, and what to do next. Plain words, real numbers, sentence case.

Overview

  • Mission Control reports on autonomous work. People open it to find out whether anything needs them, so copy leads with state and consequence: Parcel Post is blocked. Requirements failed after 3 retries. Other projects keep running.
  • Every message names the real thing: the project, the stage, the PR number, the worker, the amount. No item, resource or entity.
  • Reassure with facts, not adjectives: Agents keep working; nothing is lost, not Don’t worry!
  • These rules are extracted from the mocks; where the mocks disagreed, the decision is noted here.

  • Second person for the reader, Hewn for the product, and the agent's name only when it matters (the agent answers it and records the answer here).
  • No exclamation marks, no emoji, no jokes in errors.
  • Contractions are fine (couldn’t, isn’t); use the typographic apostrophe ’ and ellipsis ….

Sentence case

  • Everything is sentence case: page titles (New direction), buttons (Raise and resume), tabs (Design system), dialog titles (Raise Kiln Booking’s budget), table heads (Last sign-in).
  • Proper nouns keep their capitals: Hewn, Mission Control, GitHub, Magic Patterns, Greenlit, Owner and Viewer (roles), and project names (Ledgerline, Harbor CRM).
  • No all caps, including the sidebar's section labels and overlines; the overline role gives emphasis by weight and size.
  • Stage names are sentence case after their number: Stage 3 · Design system, 10 · Increments.

Buttons and verbs

Buttons are verb first and name the object when the context does not. Busy buttons switch to the progressive form with an ellipsis and keep focus.

  • The safe choice in a destructive dialog says what is kept: Keep running, not Cancel (which would be ambiguous in Cancel direction).
  • Cancel closes a form without saving; Cancel direction stops work. Do not use bare Cancel where it could mean either.
  • Links say where they go: Open the pipeline, Back to projects, Open log.

Error messages

Formula: what failed (title) + why, with the real cause and code if known + what is still fine + what to do. Field errors: what is wrong + how to fix it, in one sentence.

  • Titles are past tense and name the thing: Projects couldn’t load, Harbor CRM wasn’t deleted.
  • Give the real cause when you have it (GitHub returned 502, timeout after 10 s); it helps the Owner decide whether to wait or act.

Usage

  • Write the state first, then the consequence, then the action.
  • One idea per sentence; two sentences in a toast, three in a dialog body.
  • Keep button labels to three words; a label that wraps at 360px is too long.
  • Use the cast and numbers from the mocks in examples and tests so screenshots stay comparable.
  • When a message is announced (toast, live status), make the first words carry it: Parcel Post is blocked.

Accessibility

  • 2.4.4 / 2.4.9: link and button text makes sense out of context; repeated Open links carry the object in their accessible name (Open Live run).
  • 3.3.1 / 3.3.3: errors name the field and the fix; summaries count the problems (3 things to fix).
  • 2.4.2: every page title is unique: page, then state if any, then Hewn.
  • 4.1.3: announced text is short and self-contained, because screen readers read it without its surroundings.
  • Plain language helps everyone, including people using translation and people under stress during an outage.
